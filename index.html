How the brief picks bets

"Pass" means no bet. If the brief can't point to a specific, checkable reason that the price is wrong, it recommends nothing on that game, even if it has an opinion on who wins. Most games end up as passes; that's on purpose.

The checklist (every pick, every time)

  1. Start with the market price. No-vig sportsbook line (Market 101, Lesson 1) and the Kalshi price. Assume they're right until proven otherwise.
  2. Find a specific reason the price is off, backed by efficiency data, not just yards or records: a strength-vs-weakness mismatch (e.g., Atlanta's outside-zone runs vs. the league's worst defense against them), an injury cluster, rest or travel, a QB change.
  3. Check the history behind the headline number. Game-by-game logs, not just averages (Bijan's "116" is really 83, 72, 194). Check how the stat changes when a team is ahead or behind.
  4. Read the sharper sources (list below), and note when everyone agrees: if every preview likes the same side, the price has usually moved already.
  5. Compare Vegas and Kalshi. A gap of 3¢+ means the cheaper side gets a closer look (Lesson 7).
  6. Pick the line with cushion: +2.5 or +3 over +1.5 (3 is the most common margin); prop lines below a player's typical game, not his average.
  7. Set a top price = the brief's estimate minus a small margin. Above it, skip.
  8. Size by confidence: ½ unit for a clear reason, ¼ unit for props and combos. Make sure your bets don't work against each other.
  9. Check inactives (90 minutes before kickoff) and weather. The brief schedules this; it isn't your job.
  10. Track the closing price. If the price moves our way before kickoff, the pick found value even if it loses (Lesson 6). This is the real test of the brief over time.

Betting splits: "% of bets" vs. "% of money," and how far to trust them

What a split is: some sportsbooks publish two numbers for each side of a bet. % of bets (also called tickets) counts every bet the same, $5 or $5,000. % of money (also called handle) counts dollars. When the two numbers are far apart, they tell you who is on each side.

White bar = share of all bets (tickets) on that side. Yellow bar = share of all dollars (money, or "handle") on that side. Thursday's numbers are from CLEATZ; tonight's and Saturday's are Wednesday snapshots from one sportsbook's data via CLEATZ.

How to read it:

  1. Money % much higher than bets % = a smaller number of bigger bets. Bigger bettors are more often professionals, so books pay attention. Thursday's Bucs moneyline (18% of bets, 53% of money) is the textbook case, and Tampa won outright.
  2. Bets % much higher than money % = lots of small, casual bets. On Saturday's game, USC +1.5 has 59% of bets but only 19% of the money, so the bigger money is on Penn State, against the brief's pick.
  3. Check whether the line moved with the money. USC-Penn State flipped from USC -1.5 to Penn State -1.5: the books moved toward the big money. Once a line has moved, the money is already "in the price." That's why the brief still takes USC at +1.5: it gets the points the move created.
  4. Watch for a move against the money. This week's Browns at Jets: 90% of the money is on Cleveland, yet the line moved from Jets -1 to Jets -2.5. When a book moves the other way, it usually trusts a different, sharper group of bettors more than the crowd (reverse line movement, Lesson 3).

Why not to over-trust splits: each sportsbook only sees its own customers, so one book's split can differ from another's; a single huge bet can make "99% of money" out of one bettor; and early-week numbers change as betting limits rise. That's why the brief's checklist uses splits as a tiebreaker after a football reason, never as the reason. Tonight's Iowa-Washington under has both: a backup QB on a bad offense (the reason) and 99% of the money (the tiebreaker).

On Kalshi: Kalshi doesn't publish splits. Its price already reflects everyone's money, so use sportsbook splits to understand why a Kalshi price is moving, not to predict it.

Lesson 1: The offense's 11, and what "11 personnel" means

The fixed part: every offense has 11 players. Six never change jobs: five offensive linemen (left tackle, left guard, center, right guard, right tackle) who only block, and the quarterback (QB), who takes the snap from the center.

The flexible part: the other five are "skill players," and the coach picks the mix for each play from three types:

  • Running back (RB): lines up behind or beside the QB and carries the ball on runs. Also catches short passes and blocks blitzers.
  • Fullback (FB): a second, bigger back whose main job is to run ahead of the RB and block the first defender in the way. Many NFL teams barely use one anymore, which is why you rarely see it.
  • Tight end (TE): lines up right next to a tackle. Half blocker, half receiver.
  • Wide receiver (WR): lines up wide, near the sideline ("outside") or between the line and the outside receiver (the "slot"). Catches passes.

11 personnel (1 RB, 1 TE, so 3 WR). The most common grouping in the NFL.

21 personnel (2 backs, one of them the FB, plus 1 TE, so 2 WR). A power-running look.

The naming code: first digit = backs (RB + FB), second digit = tight ends. Subtract both from 5 to get the receivers. 11 = 1 back, 1 TE, 3 WR. 12 = 1 back, 2 TE, 2 WR. 21 = 2 backs, 1 TE, 2 WR.

Why it matters: the defense sees who jogs onto the field before every play and sends in its own matching players (Lesson 2). More blockers (TEs, a FB) usually means the offense wants to run. More receivers usually means it wants to pass. The offense's trick is to show one and do the other.

How to spot it on TV: right before the snap, count players touching the line next to a tackle (TEs), then players behind the QB (backs). Everyone split out is a WR.

Lesson 2: The defense's 11: base, nickel and dime

Three layers, front to back:

  • Defensive linemen (DL): line up nose-to-nose with the offensive line. Rush the QB or plug running lanes.
  • Linebackers (LB): stand 4–5 yards behind them. Tackle runners, cover backs and tight ends, sometimes rush the QB.
  • Defensive backs (DB): the pass defenders. Cornerbacks (CB) cover the outside receivers; safeties (S) play deep as the last line; a nickelback (NB) is a fifth DB who covers the slot receiver.

The matching game: the defense can't know the play, but it can see the offense's personnel. So it swaps players to match:

  • Base (4 DBs): against heavy groupings like 12 or 21. More linebackers to stop the run.
  • Nickel (5 DBs): against 11 personnel. One linebacker comes off, a nickelback comes on, because a linebacker is usually too slow to cover a slot receiver. (Five DBs, like the five cents in a nickel.)
  • Dime (6 DBs): on obvious passing downs like 3rd and 9. Two linebackers off, two DBs on.

Base (4 DL, 3 LB, 4 DBs) against 21 personnel. Three linebackers to meet a fullback and running back.

Nickel (4 DL, 2 LB, 5 DBs) against 11 personnel. Each receiver has a DB across from him.

Dime (4 DL, 1 LB, 6 DBs) on 3rd and long. DB = the extra defensive back.

The tradeoff: every DB added makes the defense faster against passes but smaller against runs. That's the chess match: an offense puts three receivers on the field to pull the defense into nickel, then runs the ball at the lighter group.

How to spot it on TV: count the players standing a few yards behind the defensive line. Three = base. Two = nickel. One (or none) = dime.

Lesson 3: From huddle to whistle: how one play works

Downs: the offense gets four tries ("downs") to gain 10 yards. Gain them and the count resets to "1st and 10." Fall short and the other team gets the ball, so on 4th down most teams punt or kick a field goal instead. "3rd and 4" means third try, 4 yards still to go.

The yellow line is the first-down marker you see on TV. Reach it and you get a fresh set of four downs.

Before the snap: each team gets its call from the sideline, lines up, and the offense may move players around (a "shift" or "motion") to see how the defense reacts. The QB can change the play at the line (an "audible").

A pass play is a race against the clock

From the snap, the QB has only a few seconds before pass rushers reach him. Short routes are open in about 1.5 seconds; deep routes need 2.5 seconds or more because the receiver has to run 20+ yards first. The measure that shows who's winning the race is time to throw: seconds from snap to release.

Reading the diagram: on Thursday, Atlanta's rushers hit Jordan Love 12 times on 53 pass plays, about once every four or five throws. So he got rid of the ball fast, averaging 2.26 seconds, which is before most deep routes open. That's how pressure hurts an offense even without sacks: it forces short throws, and short throws rarely produce big plays.

Why running the ball "kept Love off the field"

Only one team's offense can be on the field at a time. And the game clock keeps running after a run (if the runner is tackled in bounds), while it stops after an incomplete pass. So a drive built on runs uses up more minutes.

Atlanta ran the ball 41 times because its runs kept gaining yards. Each long, run-heavy drive burned several minutes. Every one of those minutes, Green Bay's offense, and its quarterback Love, sat on the bench. Fewer minutes with the ball means fewer drives, and fewer drives means fewer chances to score. Coaches call this "controlling the clock," and it's also why run-heavy games tend to finish under the betting total.

Run plays: two ways to block

Zone blocking: all five linemen step the same direction together and block whoever is in front of them. The RB watches where a gap opens and cuts into it. Gap blocking: the play is designed for one specific hole, and a lineman (often a guard) "pulls," running behind the line to the other side to block a defender there.

Zone blocking (inside zone). White arrows are the linemen’s first steps and the RB’s path.

Gap blocking (power). The pulling guard is the giveaway on TV: watch for a lineman running sideways behind the line.

The whistle: the play ends when the ball carrier is tackled or steps out of bounds, someone scores, or a pass hits the ground. The officials mark the spot and the next down begins.

How to read 3rd down

Look at the yellow first-down line on the TV screen. 3rd and short (1–2 yards): expect a run, because a run gains 2 yards often. 3rd and long (8+ yards): everyone knows it's a pass. The defense goes to dime (six DBs, Lesson 2) because it doesn't need run stoppers. And its pass rushers stop worrying about the run and sprint straight at the QB from the snap. Coaches call that "pinning your ears back," like a dog flattening its ears to run full speed. That's why 3rd and long is so hard to convert.

Lesson 4: Special teams: the third unit

What it is: every play with a kick. The kickoff starts each half and follows every score. The punt is a 4th-down kick that gives the ball away but pushes the other team back. The field goal is worth 3 points. The extra point is a short kick worth 1 after a touchdown; teams can instead try for 2 points with one play from the 2-yard line in the NFL (the 3 in college).

The people: kicker, punter, long snapper (hikes the ball 7 or 15 yards back, a specialist job), holder (often the punter), returners who catch kicks, and "gunners" who sprint downfield to tackle them.

Field position, connected: where a drive starts matters a lot. From your own 20, you need 80 yards to score; from midfield, 50. A great punter keeps pinning the opponent deep, so it needs longer drives, which fail more often. That's why defensive, punt-heavy games tend to be low scoring.

Yard lines count up from each goal line (G) to 50 at midfield, then back down.

How far is a field goal? Add 17 to the yard line. The ball is snapped 7 yards back to a holder, and the goalposts sit at the back of the end zone, which is 10 yards deep.

K = kicker · blue line = where the ball is

Why it matters for betting: kicks swing margins in ways the spread doesn't plan for. On Sunday, the Vikings' 86-yard punt-return touchdown was a big reason they covered, and on Thursday Atlanta blocked a Green Bay field goal. Against a 3- to 7-point spread, one blocked kick or return touchdown can decide which side wins the bet.

How to spot it on TV: after each punt or kickoff, note the yard line where the offense starts. Starts inside the 20 are a win for the kicking team.

Lesson 5: Man vs. zone: the two ways to cover receivers

Man coverage: each defender is assigned one receiver and follows him everywhere, like a shadow. It's strong when your defenders are as fast as their receivers. The weakness: if a receiver gets a step on his man, nobody else is nearby to help, so it can become a long play.

Zone coverage: each defender guards an area, not a person, and covers whoever runs into it. It's safer against long plays because some defenders always stay deep. The weakness: there are gaps between areas, and good QBs throw into those gaps.

Left: man. Dashed lines tie each defender to his receiver; one safety stays deep as backup. Right: zone. Dashed boxes are short areas, solid boxes are deep areas.

Real example: Aaron Rodgers threw touchdowns of 38 and 23 yards against Cincinnati. Long touchdowns usually come from man coverage: one receiver beats one defender, and there's no teammate behind to stop him.

Motion before the snap is the easiest man-vs-zone tell. White arrow: the receiver in motion. Yellow dashes: what the defense does.

How to spot it on TV: before the snap, watch a receiver who goes in motion across the formation. If one defender runs across with him, it's man. If nobody follows and the defenders just shuffle a step, it's zone. Lesson 8 covers the specific zone types (Cover 2, 3 and 4).

Lesson 6: The blitz: sending extra rushers is a gamble

What it is: normally four defenders rush the QB and seven cover receivers. A blitz sends a fifth or sixth rusher, usually a linebacker or DB, to reach the QB faster.

The linebacker and nickelback join the four linemen. The slot receiver the nickelback was covering is now alone, so the QB throws to him right away.

The payoff: a sack, a rushed throw or an interception. Sunday night Denver sacked Matthew Stafford four times, and the pressure led to Talanoa Hufanga's interception, returned 66 yards for a touchdown.

The risk: every extra rusher is one fewer defender in coverage. If the QB throws quickly to the spot the blitzer left (a "hot" throw), that receiver is open with nobody behind him. That's how the Rams answered minutes later with a 48-yard touchdown to Konata Mumpfield.

How to spot it on TV: before the snap, count defenders standing within a couple of yards of the line. More than four, especially linebackers creeping forward or a DB walking down from the slot, usually means a blitz. Then watch the QB: a quick throw beats it; holding the ball gets him hit.

The betting link: Steelers at Browns has the lowest total of the week, meaning the market expects few points. In low-scoring games, final margins tend to be small, so getting 2.5 points (Browns +2.5) is worth more than it would be in a shootout. Blitz-heavy defenses that force punts are part of why the total is low.

Lesson 7: How the defense adjusts in 40 seconds without knowing the play

Short answer: the defense never knows the play. It reacts to what it can see (personnel, formation, down and distance), and every defender plays by a set of rules that tell him what to do no matter which play is coming.

The 40 seconds between plays, in order:

  1. Substitution. The offense sends in its personnel (Lesson 1). If it substitutes, the rules give the defense a chance to substitute too, so the defense swaps into base, nickel or dime (Lesson 2).
  2. The call. The defensive coordinator picks a play from a sheet organized by situation: down, distance, field position, score. He radios it to one player whose helmet has a speaker, usually a linebacker (in the NFL, his helmet has a green dot on the back), who tells everyone else.
  3. Alignment. The offense lines up. The defense sets its linemen and linebackers toward the formation's "strong side" (the side with the TE or more receivers), using rules like "our best run stopper lines up across from the tight end."
  4. Checks. If the offense shifts or sends a player in motion, defenders adjust with pre-planned "checks": a one-word call that changes who covers whom. They practice these all week against the formations that opponent likes.
  5. Snap. Now each defender reads a "key," one player whose first step tells him run or pass. A linebacker watches the guard in front of him: guard fires forward = run, guard steps back = pass.

So yes, yardage drives a lot of it. Same defense, two situations:

Expecting a run: extra bodies packed near the line (the dashed "box").

Expecting a pass: extra DBs to cover, rushers ignore the run.

3rd and 1 gets a heavy box full of run stoppers; 3rd and 10 gets dime and extra rushers. But a good offense uses that against the defense, which is why play-action passes work best when the defense is sure a run is coming.

How to spot it on TV: watch the defense right as the offense breaks its huddle. You'll see players pointing and shouting. That's the checks being made. Motion right before the snap often causes a last-second scramble.

Lesson 8: Cover 2, 3 and 4: what the numbers mean

The number is how many defenders split up the deep part of the field. Not how many receivers are covered. It's the fastest way to name a coverage because the deep defenders are the ones you can see before the snap (Lesson 5's "safeties").

Cover 2: two safeties each take a deep half. Five defenders cover short areas underneath. Strong against short throws, since there are lots of bodies near the line. Soft spot: the deep middle between the two safeties, about 15–20 yards downfield.

Cover 3: two cornerbacks and one safety each take a deep third. Four defenders underneath. The other safety comes up near the line, so there's an extra run defender. Soft spot: the seams where two deep thirds meet, and short throws toward the sideline.

Cover 4 ("quarters"): four defenders each take a deep quarter. Only three underneath. Strong against deep passes. Soft spot: short throws, especially to the flats near the sideline.

The two you'll hear on TV that aren't zones:

Cover 1: man coverage (dashed ties) on every receiver, with one safety deep as backup.

Cover 0: man coverage with nobody deep, so everyone else can blitz. All or nothing.

How zone defenders keep track of receivers

  • Zones have edges, set by field markings. Each defender has "landmarks": a depth (for example, 10–12 yards for a short zone) and a width (for example, "from the hash marks to the numbers painted on the field"). He drops to that spot at the snap.
  • Eyes on the QB, not on receivers. Most zone defenders watch the QB and feel receivers entering their area. The QB's eyes and shoulders show where he's about to throw.
  • They pass receivers along. When a receiver leaves one zone and enters the next, the first defender shouts and releases him to the teammate who owns that area. That handoff is where miscommunication creates wide-open receivers.
  • Empty zone? Help next door. If nobody enters his area, he drifts toward the nearest receiver or the spot the QB is looking at.
  • Modern zones turn into man. Many teams play "match" coverage: it starts as zone, then once receivers declare their routes, each defender locks onto whoever is in his area. It combines zone's organization with man's tight coverage.

The offense's answer: send two receivers into one defender's zone at different depths. He can only cover one; the QB throws to the other. That's called "flooding" a zone.

How to spot it on TV: count the deep defenders just before the snap: two means Cover 2 or 4, one means Cover 1 or 3, none means Cover 0. Defenses do disguise by moving safeties at the snap, so check the replay's wide angle.

Lesson 9: Why bunching receivers together beats man coverage

What a bunch is: three receivers lined up in a tight triangle, a yard or two apart, instead of spread across the field.

Why it works against man: in man coverage, each defender must follow his receiver wherever he goes. When receivers start that close together and run crossing routes, the defenders chasing them have to run through the same small space. They bump into each other or get stuck behind a receiver who crossed in front of them, and their man gets a step of separation. That's called a "rub" or "pick."

It's legal, within limits: a receiver can run a route that makes a defender go around him. He can't deliberately block the defender before the ball is thrown; that's offensive pass interference.

It also blocks the "press": in man coverage, cornerbacks like to line up right on top of a receiver and jam him at the line. With three receivers stacked tightly, the defenders can't all get a clean jam without running into one another.

Why it's less effective against zone: zone defenders don't chase anyone. They stay in their areas and take whoever arrives, so crossing routes just get handed from one defender to the next (Lesson 8). Defenses also answer bunches with a "switch" call: instead of chasing through traffic, two defenders swap receivers when the routes cross.

How to spot it on TV: look for three receivers clustered near one sideline. On third down near the goal line, where teams play a lot of man, it's often the play call.

Lesson 10: Formations: where everyone lines up, and what it tells you

Personnel (Lesson 1) is who is on the field. Formation is where they stand. The same 11 players can line up many ways. Two things define a formation: where the QB stands (under center or back in shotgun), and how the receivers are spread.

T formation. The original modern formation from the 1940s. Three backs in a line behind the QB, so the run can go to either side and the defense can’t tell which back gets the ball. Today you see it at the goal line, where it’s called "full house."

I-formation. The T with the backs stacked in a straight line. The FB leads, the RB follows him through the hole. Power running; also great for play-action.

Singleback. One back behind a QB under center. Balanced: run or pass equally.

Shotgun. The QB starts 5 yards back, so he sees the defense and doesn’t need to drop back. The most common NFL alignment; leans pass but runs plenty.

Pistol. A short shotgun with the RB directly behind. The run can go either way, and the QB can run too. Common in college.

Trips (3x1). Three receivers to one side. It forces the defense to shift coverage that way, which often leaves the lone receiver on the other side one-on-one.

Bunch. Three receivers in a tight triangle. Built to beat man coverage (Lesson 9).

Empty. No back behind the QB: everyone is a receiver. Almost always a pass, and a quick one, since there’s no back to block a blitzer.

Connecting it: the closer the QB and backs are to the line and to each other (T, I), the more the offense wants to run. The more spread out everyone is (shotgun, trips, empty), the more it wants to pass. The defense reads this the same way, which is exactly why offenses sometimes run from empty or throw from the I.

Lesson 11: The two-minute drill: why the clock changes everything late in a half

The setup: each half is 30 minutes of game clock. Most of the game, the clock barely matters. In the last two minutes of a half, it matters more than anything: a team that's behind needs to move the ball and save time, while a team that's ahead wants to burn time.

What stops the clock (so the offense gets more plays):

  • An incomplete pass (the ball hits the ground).
  • A ball carrier stepping out of bounds (in the NFL, late in a half).
  • A timeout. Each team gets three per half.
  • The two-minute warning, an automatic NFL stoppage at 2:00 of each half (college doesn't have one).
  • A spike: the QB throws the ball straight into the ground on purpose. It costs a down but stops the clock.
  • College only: after a first down, the clock stops briefly while officials reset the chains. That's why college comebacks happen faster.

What keeps it running: a runner or receiver tackled in bounds. That's why, late in a half, receivers run toward the sideline and try to get out of bounds after the catch.

WR = wide receiver · QB = quarterback · dashed line = line of scrimmage · yellow circle = stepping out of bounds

Connecting it to Lesson 3: remember Atlanta running the ball to eat up the clock? The two-minute drill is the flip side. The team that's behind throws toward the sideline and stops the clock; the team that's ahead runs up the middle to keep it moving. So when you see a team leading late and running every down, that isn't timid play. It's clock management.

Real example, Monday night: Chicago kicked a field goal to go up 10-0 with 1:56 left in the first half. Philadelphia then drove the length of the field, and Jalen Hurts ran in a 1-yard touchdown with one second left before halftime. That's a textbook two-minute drill: quick throws, getting out of bounds, and using timeouts to squeeze a whole drive into under two minutes.

Why it matters for betting: "first half" and "halftime" markets on Kalshi can swing hugely in the last two minutes. The Eagles-Bears first-half total went from 10 points to 17 in the final 116 seconds. If you ever bet a first-half under, the last two minutes before halftime are the most dangerous part.

Watch for it: with under two minutes left in either half, look at the trailing team's receivers. Do they head for the sideline after catching the ball? Count the timeouts on the TV graphic and guess how many plays they'll get.

Lesson 12: The red zone: why drives stall inside the 20

What it is: the "red zone" is the last 20 yards before the end zone. Teams that get there should score, but the question is whether it's a touchdown (7) or a field goal (3). That 4-point gap decides a lot of spreads and totals.

Why it gets harder: the field shrinks. At midfield, the defense has to guard about 50 yards of depth, so there are gaps between zones (Lesson 8). At the 10-yard line, the end zone stops at the back line, so the defense only has 20 yards to guard, and the same 7 defenders are packed together.

Same 7 pass defenders (yellow), same rules. Near the goal line they have less than half the space to guard, so every receiver has a defender within a step or two.

What changes for the offense:

  • No deep throws: there's nowhere "deep" to go, so the quick, short throws and the bunch routes from Lesson 9 matter more.
  • Running gets harder too: defenses pack the box (Lesson 2) because the offense can't punish them with a long pass.
  • Big, tall receivers and QBs who can run become the most valuable players, because a jump ball or a QB scramble can beat tight coverage.

Why it matters for betting:

  • Totals: two teams that keep kicking field goals can move the ball all game and still go under. Tonight's 37.5 total assumes a lot of 3s.
  • Touchdown props: a player's TD chances depend on who gets the ball inside the 10, not on total yards. A receiver with 100 yards between the 20s may never get a goal-line target.
  • The stat to look for: "red zone TD %." Around 55–60% is average for an NFL team. Philadelphia scored TDs on 71% of red-zone trips going into Monday, Chicago 57%.

Watch for it tonight: when Pittsburgh or Cleveland gets inside the 20, guess before each play: run or pass? Then watch whether the drive ends in a TD or a field goal. Count both for each team; that's the game's red-zone story.

Lesson 13: Penalties: which flags swing games (and bets)

The basics: a yellow flag means a rule was broken. The penalty is measured in yards. Against the offense, the ball moves back toward its own goal. Against the defense, it moves forward, and many defensive penalties also give the offense an automatic first down (a fresh set of four downs, Lesson 3).

Red = penalties on the offense (ball moves back). Yellow = penalties on the defense (ball moves forward). Pass interference has no fixed number in the NFL: the ball goes to wherever the foul happened, so a deep-pass foul can be 40+ yards.

The common ones, in plain words:

  • False start (5 yards, offense): an offensive player moves before the snap. Happens most in loud road stadiums.
  • Offside / encroachment (5 yards, defense): a defender crosses the line of scrimmage before the snap.
  • Holding (10 yards): grabbing a player to stop him. On offense, it's a lineman grabbing a pass rusher. On defense, it's a DB grabbing a receiver, which also gives the offense an automatic first down.
  • Pass interference: contact that stops a player from catching a catchable pass. On the defense in the NFL, the ball goes to the spot of the foul, which makes it the biggest penalty in football.
  • Personal foul / roughing the passer (15 yards): a late or dangerous hit.

Connecting it: Thursday, Cleveland was missing two offensive linemen (Lesson 1). Backup linemen hold more, and a 10-yard holding call on 1st down turns it into 1st and 20, which kills a lot of drives. Last Monday, the Eagles' 10 penalties for 61 yards were part of why they never got going.

Why it matters for betting: a defensive pass interference or a roughing call can keep a drive alive and turn a field goal into a touchdown, which swings spreads by 4 points. Teams that commit lots of penalties are less reliable as favorites.

Watch for it tonight: Penn State's defense is missing several starters, so watch for holding and pass interference calls on its backups. That's often the first sign a thin secondary is struggling.

Lesson 14: How a defense gives up 413 rushing yards: gaps

Gaps: the spaces between offensive blockers are called gaps, lettered from the middle out: the A gap is next to the center, then B (between guard and tackle), C (outside the tackle) and D (outside the tight end). On both sides, that's up to 8 holes a runner can go through.

The defense's rule: on a run, every gap needs a defender responsible for it. Coaches call this "gap control" or "gap integrity." The linemen take some gaps; linebackers and safeties fill the rest (Lesson 2's "box" is where this happens).

Letters are the gaps (A next to the center, then B, C, D moving out). Yellow = run defenders, one per gap. Faded LB with red circle = the defender who isn't there.

How it breaks: if one defender is missing, out of position, or chasing the wrong player, his gap is empty, and a running back who reaches it is past the line with nobody in front of him except a safety. That's how runs go from 4 yards to 40.

Last night's example: Penn State played without LB Cam Smith and DE LaVar Arrington II, two of its gap-fillers. Their backups were a step late, and Northwestern's Joseph Himon II averaged 16 yards a carry. Northwestern ran for 413 yards in total and barely had to pass (14 throws).

Connecting it: once runs work, the defense has to move a safety into the box (Lesson 2's "heavy box"), which leaves fewer defenders deep, which opens the deep pass. That's why a team that can't stop the run usually can't stop anything.

Watch for it today: on a big run, rewind in your head and look at the moment the runner crossed the line: which gap did he go through, and where was the defender who should have been there?

Lesson 15: Kickoffs: the new rules and why returns matter again

What a kickoff is: the play that starts each half and follows every score. One team kicks the ball deep; the other catches it and tries to run it back.

The NFL's newer kickoff rules (the "dynamic kickoff") changed how it looks:

  • Everyone lines up close together. The kicking team (except the kicker) stands at the opponent's 40-yard line; the return team's blockers stand 5 yards away. Nobody can move until the ball lands or a returner touches it.
  • The landing zone: the kick has to land between the 20-yard line and the goal line. Inside that zone, it must be returned.
  • Kicks into the end zone are a touchback to the 35, which gives the offense good field position, so kickers try to land it short of the goal line instead.
  • Why: fewer full-speed collisions (safer), and more returns (more exciting). College still uses the older rules, with more touchbacks.

White dashed arc = the kick. Yellow solid line = where the kicking team stands; dashed = where the return team's blockers stand (5 yards apart). Yellow box = the landing zone.

Connecting it: field position (Lesson 4) starts here. A good return to the 40 instead of the 30 is 10 free yards: closer to field-goal range (Lesson 4's "add 17" math) and fewer plays needed to score.

Why it matters for betting: more returns means more chances for a long return or a return touchdown, which can swing a spread by 7 points out of nowhere. Teams with an elite returner score a little more than their offense alone suggests.

Watch for it today: on each kickoff, note where the offense starts its drive. Starting past the 35 is a win for the return team.

Lesson 16: Play-action: why a good running game makes passing easier

What it is: on a play-action pass, the quarterback fakes a handoff to the running back, then keeps the ball and throws. The fake only has to fool the defense for about one second.

Why it works: linebackers are taught to stop the run, so when they see a handoff, they step forward toward the line. That step leaves the area behind them empty, and a receiver runs into that space.

White = offense, yellow = defense. Dashed white arrows = the fake (nobody actually has the ball except the QB). Yellow arrows = the linebackers' step forward. Solid white arrow = the tight end's route into the open space, where the QB throws.

Connecting the dots:

  • It needs a real running game. If a team can't run, linebackers ignore the fake and stay back. That's why play-action is deadliest for teams like tonight's Falcons: after Bijan Robinson gains 6, 8, 12 yards on runs, the linebackers have to step up.
  • It beats the safeties too. If the safety (S) also creeps up to help against the run, there's no one deep, and play-action becomes a long touchdown. That's the "big play" risk for a defense that's selling out to stop the run.
  • It protects the QB. The fake freezes pass rushers for a moment, too, giving the QB more time (connects to Lesson 6 on pressure and blitzes).
  • Defenses fight it by having linebackers read the offensive linemen instead of the running back: linemen who pop up to pass-block give away that it's a pass.

Why it matters for betting: a strong running game helps the passing game too. That's why the brief lists Drake London as a reasonable "yards" lean tonight even though the main reason to like Atlanta is the run game.

Lesson 17: Goal line and short yardage: why a different back scores the touchdowns

The situation: the ball is on the 1, 2 or 3-yard line, or it's 3rd/4th and 1 anywhere. The offense needs a yard or two, and the defense knows it.

Both teams pack in. The offense brings in extra blockers: two tight ends and a fullback ("22 personnel," Lesson 1). The defense answers with eight or nine players crowded near the line (a "heavy box," Lesson 2), because there's no deep field left to protect (Lesson 12).

White = offense, yellow = defense. White squares = offensive linemen. Dashed yellow box = the 9 defenders near the line. The arrow is the power back's run straight into the A gap (Lesson 14), the space next to the center.

Why a different back: near the goal line, speed matters less, because there's no open field to outrun anyone. What matters is power (pushing a pile forward), ball security (a fumble here costs 7 points), and fresh legs. Many teams use a bigger "goal-line back" for these carries and save their star for the open field.

Monday's example: Bijan Robinson (the explosive back) scored from 59 yards on his first carry. But Brian Robinson Jr. (the bigger back) got the goal-line work: 3 touchdowns from close range, including two from the 4. That split is why Bijan finished at 145 yards and 2 TDs instead of more, and why the 160+ bet missed.

Connecting the dots:

  • Play-action again (Lesson 16): because defenses expect a run here, goal-line play-action to a tight end is one of the most common touchdown plays in football.
  • Quarterback sneaks: on 3rd or 4th and 1, many teams just have the QB push forward behind the center. It's the highest-success short-yardage play.
  • Gaps (Lesson 14): with eight defenders in the box, every gap is filled. The offense wins only by moving bodies backward, which is why the biggest linemen matter most here.

Why it matters for betting: for a running back's touchdown or very high yardage props, check who gets carries inside the 5-yard line, not just who gets the most carries. A star back who shares goal-line work is a better bet for yards than for touchdowns.

Lesson 18: Third down: why the "money down" is decided on first and second down

The basics: the offense has four downs to gain 10 yards (Lesson 3). Fourth down is usually a punt or kick, so third down is the last real try. Convert it and the drive lives; fail and the other team gets the ball.

Bar height = roughly how often NFL offenses convert from each distance. These are rounded league-wide figures to show the pattern, not exact numbers for any team.

The key idea: "staying on schedule." Third-and-2 is a coin flip in the offense's favor; third-and-9 is a long shot. So the real fight is on first and second down. A 4-yard run on first down leaves 2nd-and-6, then 3rd-and-short. A sack or an incomplete leaves 3rd-and-long.

What the defense does on 3rd-and-long: it knows a pass is coming, so it brings in extra defensive backs (dime, Lesson 2) and often blitzes (Lesson 6), aiming to force a quick, short throw that ends short of the marker.

Tonight's example: Tampa Bay's defense is top-5 on third down; Dallas's is 31st. Tampa's rookie QB Jalon Daniels will face more blitzes on 3rd-and-long, but Dallas's poor third-down defense gives him a better chance than most rookies. Watch the distance on each third down: if Tampa keeps facing 3rd-and-7+, it will struggle to cover.

Connecting the dots: third-down success keeps drives (and the clock, Lesson 11) going; red-zone success (Lesson 12) turns those drives into touchdowns instead of field goals. Together they decide most spreads.

Why it matters for betting: teams that convert third downs run more plays, so their QB's attempts and their total points go up. A strong third-down defense is one of the best reasons to take an underdog with points.

Lesson 19: Count the box: the pre-snap clue for run or pass

The box is the rectangle right in front of the offensive line: about 5 yards deep and as wide as the tight ends (Lesson 2 introduced it). Defenders standing in it are there to stop the run. Defenders outside it (cornerbacks, deep safeties) are there for passes.

The count: before the snap, count the defenders in the box and compare them with the offense's blockers: the 5 linemen plus any tight end or fullback. The running back and QB don't count; they carry or hand off the ball.

White = offense (squares = the 5 linemen), yellow = defense. Dashed yellow rectangle = the box. Top: 4 linemen + 2 linebackers = 6 in the box, matching the 6 blockers. Bottom: a third linebacker moves inside and a safety walks down: 8 in the box, more than the 6 blockers can handle.

What the count tells you:

  1. Blockers ≥ box defenders (a "light box"): the offense can block everyone, so runs work. Offenses with a good QB often check (change the play at the line) into a run when they see this.
  2. Box defenders > blockers (a "loaded" or "stacked" box, usually 8): someone will be unblocked on a run. But to get that 8th man, the defense pulls a safety out of deep coverage, leaving one safety deep. That's when passes and play-action (Lesson 16) work best.
  3. The catch: defenses disguise. A safety can stand at 12 yards and sprint down at the snap, or walk down early and back out. Count once when the offense lines up and again just before the snap.

Real examples this week:

  • Thursday, Bucs 24, Cowboys 16: Dallas played without LB DeMarvion Overshown, one of its main box defenders. Tampa ran 39 times for 241 yards (6.2 a carry) and kept the ball 39:21, so its rookie QB only had to throw 25 times. When a defense is short of run-stoppers, it has to choose: leave the box light and give up runs, or pull a safety down and risk deep passes.
  • Penn State at Northwestern (413 rushing yards): PSU was missing LB Cam Smith and DE LaVar Arrington II, two of its box players, and Northwestern ran through the empty gaps (Lesson 14).
  • Saturday's test: USC at Penn State (4:30 p.m., NBC): Penn State is still missing those players. If it stacks the box with a safety to stop USC's run, QB Jayden Maiava (9.1 yards per pass) gets one-on-one throws outside. If it stays light, USC can run. Either way, that's the reason behind the brief's USC +1.5.

Connecting the dots: personnel (Lesson 1) tells you how many blockers there are; the box count tells you whether the defense matched them; third down (Lesson 18) is where defenses most often load the box or empty it, because they know the distance.

For your Film Room drill: before each snap, add one step: count the box. More blockers than box defenders → lean run. Eight in the box with one deep safety → lean pass or play-action. Market 101 · Lesson 1: Implied probability and the book's cut

American odds are a win chance in disguise. For a favorite, divide the odds by the odds plus 100: Penn State at -420 is 420 / 520 = 80.8%. For an underdog, divide 100 by the odds plus 100: Wisconsin at +320 is 100 / 420 = 23.8%.

Those add to 104.6%. A fair coin can't be 52% heads and 52% tails, so the extra 4.6% is the sportsbook's built-in cut, called the vig. It's why you need to win more than half your bets just to break even.

On Kalshi, a contract at 81 cents means the market sees an 81% chance, and a correct contract pays $1. The fee is charged separately instead of hiding inside the price.

Market 101 · Lesson 2: The point spread, and why -110 is everywhere

A spread handicaps the favorite. "Packers -4.5" means Green Bay must win by 5 or more for that bet to cash. "Falcons +4.5" wins if Atlanta loses by 4 or fewer, or wins outright. The half point exists so the bet can't end in a tie, which is called a push.

The standard price on each side is -110: you risk $110 to win $100. Using Lesson 1's formula, 110 / 210 = 52.4%. So to break even betting spreads, you have to win 52.4% of the time, not 50%. That 2.4% is the vig at work.

On Kalshi, the same bet is a contract like "Packers win by 6+." If it trades at 51¢, you pay 51¢ to win $1, which beats -110's 52.4¢ equivalent before fees. That's the kind of gap the price board is hunting for.

Market 101 · Lesson 3: Why lines move

A sportsbook's opening line is its best guess. After that, three things move it:

News. Injuries and QB changes. Thursday's line dropped from 7 to 4.5 when Penix was confirmed as the starter and Green Bay's injuries piled up. That move turned out to be right; Atlanta won by 21.

Money, and whose money. Books care less about how much is bet than who bets it. Professional bettors ("sharps") have long track records of beating closing lines, so books move quickly when sharps bet. Casual bettors ("the public") tend to back favorites and Overs, and books often let that money sit.

Key numbers. Football margins bunch up at 3 and 7 because of field goals and touchdowns. A move from USC -1.5 to Oregon -3 isn't just 4.5 points; it crossed zero and landed on 3, which is a big statement. Books move off 3 reluctantly.

The tell: when most bets are on one team but the line moves the other way, that's called reverse line movement, and it usually means sharp money is on the other side.

Market 101 · Lesson 4: Parlays, and why books love them

A parlay ties several bets together: every leg has to win, and the payout multiplies. Two -110 legs pay about +264; three pay about +596. Big numbers, which is the appeal.

The catch is that the vig multiplies too. Each -110 leg needs a 52.4% win rate to break even, so two legs need 52.4% × 52.4% = 27.4%, and three need 14.4%. A true coin-flip bettor hits a two-leg parlay 25% of the time, below that break-even. That gap is why books earn a bigger cut on parlays than on straight bets.

When a parlay makes sense: when the legs are correlated, meaning one leg winning makes the other more likely. Thursday's Falcons +5.5 with the Under was correlated: a low-scoring game helps the underdog stay close. Today's Oklahoma and Iowa parlay isn't correlated, which is why it's sized at half a unit.

On Kalshi: combos are priced by multiplying the contract prices. Two 50¢ outcomes cost about 25¢ combined, so the same math applies, minus fees.

Market 101 · Bonus: Kalshi combos: Four things to know

1. Kalshi's price is an offer, not a set formula. When you build a combo, other traders on Kalshi offer you a price. The "fair price" is just the individual prices multiplied together (56¢ × 55¢ = 30.8¢). Anything above that is extra cost to you, like the house's cut at a sportsbook.

2. Keep combos short. Two or three bets is best. The more you add, the fewer traders offer a price, and the worse the price gets.

3. Same-game combos (two bets on one game, like Combo 3) only work on games Kalshi has opened for it, which often happens close to kickoff.

4. No do-overs if a player sits. At a sportsbook, if a player in your parlay doesn't play, that piece usually just gets dropped. On Kalshi, it can instead settle for part of its value, which shrinks your payout. It's marked with a blue arrow in the app.

Market 101 · Lesson 5: Units: how not to go broke while being right

The idea: pick one amount as your "unit" and bet that same amount (or a set fraction of it) every time. Most people who track betting seriously use 1–2% of their total betting money as a unit. So if you set aside $500 for the season, a unit is $5 to $10.

Why it matters: even good bettors lose often. Someone who wins 55% of their bets, which is very good, will still hit losing streaks of 5 or 6 in a row. If you bet big after a loss to "win it back," one bad streak can wipe you out. Same-size bets let a good record actually show up in your results.

How the brief uses it: 1 unit for the picks I like most, half a unit for parlays and combos (they lose more often), and a quarter unit for long shots. This morning is a good example: the Chase TD was only a quarter unit, but it still paid 0.33 units because it was priced at 43¢.

A simple rule: never raise your unit because you're behind. Raise it only after a long stretch of good results, and lower it if your total pot shrinks.

Market 101 · Lesson 6: Closing line value: were you right before the game even started?

The idea: the "closing line" is the last price before kickoff. By then every injury report, weather update and big bettor has pushed the price to its most accurate point. If you consistently buy at a better price than the close, you're beating the market, even on bets that lose.

Your weekend, as an example: you bought Davante Adams to score at about 46¢. Earlier Sunday he was 43¢, and after Nacua was ruled out the fair price should have been higher. If Adams closed at, say, 50¢, you "beat the close" by 4¢. The bet still lost, but the decision was good. Do that 100 times and the wins show up.

The other direction: the Vikings add-on at about 56¢ came right after Tampa scored, when the price had already moved up. Buying after a move usually means paying the new, less generous price.

Market 101 · Lesson 7: Why popular teams cost extra on Kalshi, and how to use it

The idea in one line: a Kalshi price is set by whoever is buying, not by a bookmaker. When lots of casual fans pile onto a famous, popular team, they push its price higher than its real chance of winning. That extra is called a "public premium."

How to spot it (three steps):

  1. Get the sportsbook price and remove the cut. Example from Monday: the Eagles closed at -186 and the Bears at +178. Those turn into 65.0% and 36.0%, which add to 101% (the extra 1% is the book's cut from Lesson 1). Divide each by 101% to get the fair numbers: Eagles about 64%, Bears about 36%.
  2. Compare to Kalshi. Kalshi had the Eagles at 69¢, about 5¢ above fair.
  3. Flip it. If one side is 5¢ too expensive, the other side is about that much too cheap. The Bears were roughly 31–32¢ on Kalshi against a fair ~36¢.

What happened: the Bears won 27-7. One game proves nothing; the point is that you'd have been paying less than fair. Do that over and over and it adds up, exactly like the "beat the close" idea from Lesson 6.

Where the premium usually shows up: prime-time games (everyone's watching), famous teams (Eagles, Cowboys, Chiefs, Ohio State, Alabama), "Yes" on overs and touchdown scorers, and the favorite in a game with a big-name QB. Fans rarely rush to buy "Under" or "No."

This week's example: Kalshi has the Steelers at 59¢ Thursday vs. about 57% fair at the sportsbooks. That's only a 2¢ gap, which is noise by the rule below, so the brief's Browns lean rests on football reasons, not price.

The rule of thumb: a 1–2¢ gap is noise. At 3¢ or more, lean toward the cheaper side, and only if the football reasons agree.

Market 101 · Lesson 8: Live betting: when a mid-game price is a deal, and when it's just a reaction

The idea: Kalshi keeps trading during the game, so prices jump after every big play. A live bet is worth it only when the price moved more than the play actually changed the game.

Ask two questions before buying live:

  1. Did the play change who's better, or just the score? A fluky touchdown (a tipped interception, a return TD) changes the score but not which team is stronger. An injury to a starting QB changes both.
  2. How much time is left? Early in a game, a 7-point lead is worth much less than it feels. A team down 7 in the 1st quarter still wins roughly a third of the time.

Real example: Monday night, the Eagles were down 7-0 early. Their price dropped, but nothing about the teams had changed. That's the kind of "dip" that can be a buy. Market 101 · Lesson 12: Weather, wind and totals: which conditions actually lower scoring

The short version: of all the weather, wind is the one that matters most. Rain and cold sound scary but move scoring much less than people think, unless they're extreme.

  2. Long field goals get harder, so drives that would end in 3 points end in punts or 4th-down tries instead.

Market 101 · Lesson 13: Combo math: why you multiply, and when the legs move together

Start with Lesson 1: a Kalshi price is a chance. 51¢ means 51%. A combo contract pays $1 only if every leg wins, so its fair price is the chance that all the legs win.

Why multiply: picture tonight's game played 100 times.

  1. Falcons +1.5 is 51¢, so Atlanta covers in about 51 of the 100 games.
  2. The combo also needs Bijan 70+ yards in those same 51 games. If his chance is 67% no matter what, he gets there in 67% of 51 ≈ 34 games.
  3. 34 of 100 = 34%, so the fair price is 34¢. Multiplying (51% × 67%) is just a shortcut for "a percentage of a percentage."

Each square is one game. White + yellow = the 51 games where the Falcons cover. Yellow = the games where Bijan also gets 70+, which is when the combo pays. Faded = the 49 games the Falcons don't cover.

How price becomes cost: at 34¢, $10 buys about 29.4 contracts, paying $29.41. Kalshi's offer of $24.40 for $10 works out to $10 ÷ $24.40 = 41¢. So you'd be paying 41¢ for something worth 34¢ if the legs were unrelated.

When the legs move together: they aren't unrelated. The games where Atlanta covers are mostly games where its run game worked, and a team that's ahead runs even more to burn the clock (Football 101, Lessons 3 and 11). So within those 51 games, Bijan's chance of 70+ is higher than his overall 67%. The brief estimates about 78%. 78% of 51 ≈ 40 games, or 40%. That's the right-hand grid.

Where 78% comes from: it's a judgment call, not a published number. Reasonable people could say 75% or 82%, which moves the combo between about 38% and 42%. To check a number like this yourself, look at a player's game logs and compare his stats in games his team won or covered vs. games it didn't.

The decision: Kalshi wants 41¢; the brief's best estimate is 40%. No discount, so skip the combo and bet the two legs on their own. It also shows Kalshi already priced the link in: 41¢ is well above the 34¢ "unrelated" price.

The rule: a combo is worth buying only when its price is clearly (about 3¢+) below your estimate of the linked chance. If the legs are unrelated, the multiplied price is the ceiling.

Market 101 · Lesson 14: Following a line from Monday's open to Sunday's kickoff

How a week of betting works: sportsbooks post "opening" lines Sunday night or Monday, with low betting limits. Professional bettors hit the numbers they think are wrong early in the week; books adjust. News (injuries, weather) moves lines any day. Casual money arrives late, mostly on weekends.

Each line is a point spread. Toward the left, the first team is favored; toward the right, the second team. White dot = opening line; yellow dot = Wednesday. Yellow bands = the key numbers 3 and 7 (Market 101, Lesson 3), the most common final margins.

Three kinds of moves, all from this week:

  1. News move (Ravens at Falcons): Lamar Jackson's ankle took the Ravens from 6-point favorites to 3-point underdogs, a 9-point swing. Moves this big only happen for a starting QB (Lesson 11's 3–7 points per QB, and Lamar is worth more than most).
  2. Crossing zero (USC at Penn State): USC -1.5 became Penn State -1.5. The favorite changed. With no injury news, that's usually money on the home team; the efficiency data disagrees with the move, which is why the brief now likes USC more, getting points instead of giving them.
  3. Moving through a key number (Texans at Titans): -3.5 to -7 jumps past 4, 5 and 6 to the next key number. Books are slow to cross 3 and 7 because so many games land there, so a move through one is a strong signal.

How to use it on Kalshi:

  1. If you like a side because of news, bet early, before the line catches up.
  2. If you like the side the public is betting against (like USC this week), wait: popular-side money often pushes your price better by Saturday.
  3. Note the price when you buy and again at kickoff (closing-price tracker, under "How picks are made"). Beating the close is the best sign a pick was good, win or lose.

Market 101 · Lesson 15: Player props: how the yardage ladder is priced, and why touchdowns are different

The yardage ladder: on Kalshi, a player's yards come as a ladder of "X+" contracts (60+, 70+, 80+, ...). Each rung is its own bet. The higher the rung, the less likely it is, so it costs less and pays more.

Yellow bar = Kalshi's price (the market's chance) for each rung before Monday's game. Right column = what $10 pays if it hits. These are the real prices you saw.

How to pick a rung:

  1. Find the sportsbook's single line (Bijan's was 86.5). That's the market's middle: about a 50/50 rung.
  2. Compare it to the player's typical game (his middle game), not his average. Bijan's average was 116 but his typical game was about 83, because one huge game pulled the average up.
  3. Pick the rung where your reason is strongest. A matchup edge (Saints missing run-stoppers) pushes the whole ladder up a bit, which helps the lower rungs most, since they only need a normal game.

Why touchdowns are different: yards pile up all game, so a good player usually gets close to his number. A touchdown is one moment, and it depends on who gets the ball inside the 5 (Football 101, Lesson 17). Monday showed it:

Bijan had more than twice the yards, but Brian Robinson Jr. scored more touchdowns.

Rules of thumb: yards props reward a matchup edge; TD props need a goal-line role. Prices on famous players' TDs carry a "public premium" (Lesson 7), so "Yes, anytime TD" on a star is usually a little expensive.
